# Viva Preparation

Questions an examiner is likely to ask, answered from what this project actually
measured. Where a number appears here it is in the artifacts too; where something
was not measured, the answer says so.

The three hardest questions are at the end. They are the ones worth rehearsing.

---

## Method and design

**1. Why PPO rather than DQN or SAC?**

PPO is on-policy, stable under the non-stationarity of many agents learning at
once, and works directly with a discrete phase action. DQN's replay buffer holds
transitions generated by *other* agents' older policies, which is exactly the
data that goes stale fastest in a multi-agent signal problem. SAC is built for
continuous control, which this action space is not. PPO also has trustworthy
implementations in both libraries this project uses (Stable-Baselines3 for
single-agent, RLlib for multi-agent), so the algorithm is not a confound.

**2. What does the Lagrangian fairness constraint actually compute?**

For each junction it takes the worst approach lane's **mean per-vehicle** waiting
time — the lane's summed accumulated wait divided by its occupancy — and
penalises the amount by which that exceeds a cap:

```
violation   = max(0, worst_lane_mean_wait - max_wait_s)
penalty     = lambda * fairness_scale * (violation / max_wait_s)
```

Two details matter and both were arrived at by fixing a bug. Dividing by the cap
makes the violation dimensionless, so retuning the cap does not silently rescale
the penalty. `fairness_scale` (0.05) puts the penalty in the delay term's
per-step units: the delay term telescopes over an episode while this penalty
accumulates every step, and without the conversion it dominated the return by two
orders of magnitude — the first version produced a return of −163,388. `lambda`
is not a fixed weight; it rises by dual ascent while the constraint is violated
and decays when it is satisfied.

**3. How does the GNN encode neighbour state?**

Each signalised junction is a node; corridor connectivity gives the edges. A
graph-attention encoder builds a per-sample star graph around each junction and
attends over its neighbours' observations, producing an embedding that replaces
the raw local observation. Observations are padded **field by field** — phase
one-hot, min-green flag, density, queue — so that index *k* means the same thing
at a 3-way junction with 9 features as at a 4-way one with 11. Naive flat padding
would have silently misaligned them.

**4. Why is the LLM service read-only, and how is that enforced?**

Because a hallucinated token must never be able to change a signal. It is
enforced three ways, none of which is a comment: `src/test_week7.py` imports the
service in a clean subprocess and fails if any simulation or RL module appears in
`sys.modules`; the service's public surface is asserted to be exactly `ask` and
`close`; and `tests/test_services.py` reads the deployed service's own OpenAPI
schema and fails if any mutating route exists.

## Results

**5. What is the headline result?**

On the 12-junction corridor at base demand, 3-seed means: fixed-time waits
83.1 s, SUMO's actuated controller 40.9 s, and the multi-agent RL policy 17.8 s.
That is 79% below fixed-time and 56% below a genuinely strong classical baseline.

**6. Which Definitions of Done failed?**

Three of them. Week 3 (single-junction RL beat fixed-time on all three metrics
but the actuated baseline on only one), Week 5 (beat actuated on wait but not
reliably on throughput — one seed of three jammed the corridor — and the fairness
constraint changed nothing measurable), and Week 9's federated learning, which
improved a held-out district by exactly 0.0%.

**7. Why did the fairness constraint do nothing?**

Because of the saturation described below: the constraint changed the reward the
policy was optimising without changing any decision the policy could act on.
Measured, not inferred — `wait_p95_s` is 48.0 s both with and without it.

**8. Is the detector result trustworthy?**

The number is real — mAP50 0.988 on a junction held entirely out of training —
but it should not be read as camera-ready performance. The frames are rendered
from the simulation: no occlusion, no perspective, no motion blur, no weather on
the lens. UA-DETRAC would have provided real footage; it is behind a registration
wall and was not used. The right claim is "the detection pipeline works end to
end", not "this model can watch a road".

**9. Preemption helped emergency vehicles *and* general traffic. Isn't that
suspicious?**

It is worth being suspicious of, yes. Emergency wait fell 86% (416 s → 58 s) and
general traffic wait fell slightly rather than rising. The mechanism is specific
to a saturated corridor: an emergency vehicle stopped at a red is itself an
obstruction, holding a lane while a queue builds behind it. Clearing it early
removes that blockage. On a lightly loaded network the trade would almost
certainly go the other way, and this result should not be generalised past the
demand it was measured at.

## The three hard questions

**10. Five different networks produced identical results. Is your experiment
broken?**

No, and this is the project's most interesting finding. Five Week 5 variants — no
fairness, 20× fairness, no coordination, a graph-attention encoder instead of an
MLP — produce byte-identical metrics despite having genuinely different
architectures (16k parameters against 138k). Week 6 measured why: they agree on
**100% of the decisions that can actually move a signal**. The one variant that
disagrees at all, `w5strong`, disagrees 21% of the time, and every one of those
disagreements falls inside sumo-rl's minimum-green window, where the decision is
discarded before it reaches the simulator.

Week 9 then found the same thing from a completely independent direction. Three
districts each trained a policy on their own junction, on their own traffic.
Their weights genuinely diverge — mean L2 distance 1.41 — yet they agree on
**100% of decisions** on a held-out junction, so federated averaging improves on
them by exactly nothing.

The cause is the action space, not any of the algorithms: a binary next-phase
action with a 5-second minimum green leaves so little room that almost every
reasonable policy collapses onto the same greedy rule. The fix is
phase-duration control, and it is the clearest next piece of work here.

**11. You beat the baselines by a lot. Why should anyone believe it?**

Four reasons, and the fourth is the one that matters. Every controller is
measured by the same per-second collector on identical traffic through the same
SUMO options. Every reported result is a 3-seed mean. The baselines are not straw
men — the actuated controller has explicit `minDur`/`maxDur` on its green phases,
without which SUMO silently degrades it into fixed-time, and it is genuinely
strong. And the project reports the cases where RL *loses*: `BENCHMARK_REPORT.md`
enumerates every (scenario, baseline, metric) combination and keeps the losing
ones, generated from the data so an unflattering cell cannot be quietly dropped.

**12. What would you do differently, and what is the biggest weakness?**

The biggest weakness is that everything runs on one synthetic grid with a
synthetic demand generator. The OSM corridor was deferred in Week 1 and never
picked up, so nothing here demonstrates transfer to a real network — and that
single fact bounds every number in the project.

What I would do differently: refine the action space before spending Weeks 5 and
6 on reward shaping. Two weeks went into green-wave shaping and a fairness
constraint that provably could not change behaviour, and the ablation that
revealed this was run *afterwards*. Running the policy-agreement check first
would have redirected that effort into phase-duration control, which is where the
remaining headroom actually is.
